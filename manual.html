1. The one idea behind it

The app shows you a word. You try to remember it. You say how that went. It works out when to show you the word again — soon if you struggled, much later if you didn't.

That's the whole system. Everything else is detail.

The reason it spaces things out is that a word you review the moment before you'd have forgotten it sticks far better than one you review while it's still fresh. Reviewing something you already know solidly is close to wasted effort. So when the app tells you a word isn't due for two months, it isn't neglecting it — it's saying you don't need it yet, and it's usually right.

2. The buttons

After you reveal the answer, you tell the app how it went. Four buttons, always the same four, in the same order, on every word — whether it is brand new, being relearned after a slip, or settled into its rhythm.

The four are a ladder of how much effort the recall took — and only the bottom rung is a failure:

AgainYou didn't recall it. The only failing grade. If you're not sure you recalled it, it's Again.
HardYou recalled it, but only just — a real struggle. A pass, a weak one.
GoodYou recalled it, with a moment's thought — the normal case.
EasyIt was there instantly, no thought.

Each button shows the interval it will produce, so you can see the consequence before you commit to it. Hard is not "I want to see this sooner", and not "I recognised it once I saw the answer" — that second one is Again. The line between Hard and Good is how hard you had to dig, not whether you got there: both got there. (A one-line reminder of this sits above the card at the start of each sitting until your first rating — the Grade reminder setting, §8, on by default; switch it off if it has done its job.)

What each button does, in each state

A word is in one of three states: learning (brand new, walking its short steps of 1 minute then 10 minutes), relearning (it slipped in review and gets one 10-minute look), or review (settled, coming back after days or weeks). This table is the whole rule for what a grade does. (The one thing that can change which grade is recorded — a slow Easy becoming Good — is in §3.)

Learning (1m, 10m steps)Relearning (one 10m step)Review
AgainBack to the 1-minute step.Stays on the 10-minute step.A lapse: back for one 10-minute look, then review again.
HardAdvances a step, and from the last step graduates, exactly as Good does — but the word is marked harder, so it comes back sooner from then on.Graduates back to review, as Good does, marked harder.A shorter interval than Good.
GoodAdvances a step; from the last step, graduates.Graduates back to review.The normal interval.
EasyGraduates at once, skipping the steps.Graduates back to review, stronger.A longer interval than Good.

Every button except Again moves the word forward. So no word can ever get stuck circling the same short step: press Hard honestly on a word you are half-getting and it still graduates on the second press, just weaker — the app tracks that it was a struggle and brings it back sooner in review. This is a deliberate departure from Anki, where Hard on a learning step repeats the step (so a single-step deck can never graduate a card its user honestly rates Hard — an open request on the Anki forums since 2024). It is also why there is no "after N Hards, do X" rule anywhere: a rule like that would only be needed if a button could fail to advance, and none can.

Hard, precisely

Because Hard is the button most worth getting right, here is exactly what it does, in order of when you'll meet it. It never does anything else.

  • First press on a brand-new word: the word comes back in 10 minutes, the same as Good — but it starts out weaker (about a day's strength, where a Good starts it at about two days) and marked harder.
  • Second press, same day (the 10-minute step): the word graduates into review, the same as Good, and to the same first interval as Good would give — usually "tomorrow" for a word that was Hard on its first press, about two days for a word that was Good first. What differs is the difficulty the word now carries, which shortens every interval from the second review on. (That tie is the scheduler's own rule for same-day repeats, explained just below.)
  • On a word relearning after a slip (the single 10-minute look): graduates it straight back to review, the same as Good, marked harder.
  • In review, on a settled word: a shorter interval than Good — roughly three-quarters of it, and the gap widens as the word matures (the table in §4 gives the shape).
  • What it never does: it never repeats a step, never sends a word back to one minute, never counts toward setting a word aside (only Again does that, §9), and is never rewritten by the timing rule in §3 — a Hard you press is a Hard that's recorded.
  • When it comes back, grade it again on its own merits. A word that was Hard last time and comes easily this time is Good or Easy now; the app already remembers that it was a struggle once.

One thing worth knowing so it doesn't look like a bug: on the second learning press of the same day, Hard and Good often show the same first interval. That is the scheduler's own rule for same-day repeats (a pass never lowers its belief in a memory, so neither Hard nor Good changes it much minutes after the first look), and it is what every reference version of the scheduler does. The difference is real all the same — Hard marks the word as harder, and a harder word grows its intervals more slowly at every review after that — it just shows up from the second review on, not on the first.

The only way to break this

Press Easy on a word you actually half-guessed. Everything the app does rests on your grades being honest. If you're generous, it schedules words too far out, you forget them, and you'll conclude the app doesn't work. Being harsh on yourself costs you a few extra reviews. Being generous costs you the word.

The rule for words with several meanings (restated 2026-08-19, after a looser one-sense rule was tried in this paragraph for a day and withdrawn): recall every meaning listed, or it's Again. The list was assigned to be known whole, and "I know one of the four" is not knowing the word. That holds whether the meanings are separate senses (found, establish, store up, hide), several English attempts at one sense (thing, affair, matter), or two words that only mark a range together (by, from) — the app does not yet tell these apart, so the card is the unit. An extra meaning that genuinely belongs to the word costs you nothing. If what came to mind actually belongs to a different word, that's a mix-up between cards, not a lapse on this one: grade this card on its own meanings, then flag both words (§10) and group them from Browse — the app will start quizzing them side by side instead of letting them keep bleeding into each other.

3. Two things that will surprise you

A new word comes back in ten minutes, not tomorrow

Press Good on a word you're meeting for the first time and it comes back in ten minutes. If nothing else is left to review, it may come back sooner rather than leave you with an empty screen. Press Good again on that second look and it graduates — the ten-minute gap is the same-day survival check, and passing it is enough. Hard walks the same steps and graduates the same way, only starting the word off weaker (§2 has the full table). Easy skips the check and graduates it at once. Again sends it back to one minute. A word you've just failed gets one ten-minute look the same way, then goes back into rotation. (Until 2026.08.19.1 a Good was not enough — the word kept circling every ten minutes until you pressed Easy or it ran out of same-day tries. That rule is gone, deleted along with the settings that drove it; see the changelog.)

This only affects words being learned or relearned. A word that's settled into a normal rhythm behaves normally — Good moves it along, Hard moves it along a little less, Easy a little more.

A slow Easy is recorded as Good

The app times the gap between seeing the word and revealing the answer. It never shows a clock and never interrupts you. There is one rule: take more than five seconds and an Easy is recorded as Good.

Nothing else is ever touched. Good stays Good however long you take, Hard stays Hard, and Again is never altered — nor is anything ever recorded higher than what you pressed. When a grade does get adjusted, a brief message tells you. If you put the phone down mid-card, that card's timing is discarded rather than counted against you. You can switch the whole thing off in settings. How often it actually fires is counted, from 2026.08.19.2 on, under Diagnostics in the stats page's Calibration panel (§5) — and that panel lists it as the only thing that ever changes a press before the scheduler sees it. If a second line ever appears there, something has been put back in between.

The reasoning: "that was effortless" is the one claim a clock can check and no button can. Whether a pass was a struggle is a different question, and you answer that yourself with Hard (§2) — so the app stopped guessing at it.

Skipping the reveal on a word you already know — "Quick rate"

Off unless you switch it on, in Settings, under Quick rate. With it on, a word you know the instant you see it can be graded without revealing it: press 4 on a keyboard, or tap the Knew it button that appears beside Show answer on a phone. That records Easy and moves straight to the next word.

Three things worth knowing before you turn it on. It only appears on a word past its first learning steps — never on one you are still learning, which is the place where grading a word you don't really know quietly costs the most. Only Easy can be pressed this way: 1, 2 and 3 do nothing from the question side, and there is no equivalent on the phone, because Hard and Good are the two grades that mean "I actually looked at it", and an Again you could press without looking would tell you nothing you didn't already know. And the five-second rule still applies (just above): press it slowly and the app records Good, exactly as it would after a reveal. So that this is never invisible, the answer flashes up straight afterwards with the grade it recorded underneath — "Easy", or "Good — that took a moment".

Got one wrong? On a keyboard, Z pressed immediately after corrects that word to Again in a single stroke — not an undo, a correction, and it schedules exactly as any other Again does. On a phone, undo with the arrow at the top right, then reveal and press Again.

A word graded this way never shows its card note, since it never reveals. That is deliberate rather than an omission: a mnemonic is for a word you did not have, and a word you knew instantly did not need one. If the flash tells you that you were wrong and you correct it, the word comes back within the minute through the ordinary reveal, note and all.

(This existed from 2026.08.07, was switched off for everyone on 2026.08.19.1, and came back in this narrower shape on 2026.08.20.1 — keyboard-only until then, and with two more modes and three more grades than it has now. The changelog has the reasoning.)

The study-screen timer

Since 2026.08.19.5 the same clock also keeps a sitting log: each stretch it runs — from walking into the study screen to leaving it — is recorded as one sitting, with when it started, when it stopped and how many ratings you made in it (split at the day boundary if it crosses one; a stretch under thirty seconds with no rating is not counted). Nothing shows it yet. It exists so that, after a few weeks of sittings, the stats page can answer two questions in a sentence each — whether words learnt in a long sitting survive their first review as well as words from a short one, and whether your grading goes better at some hours than others — which need sittings recorded, not just days. And when the queue runs out, the Done screen asks one thing: How did that feel? light · fine · heavy. One tap, or none; it stores a single answer for the day, shown back on the calendar's day panel, and is the only direct measurement of "manageable" the app can ever have — it is what turns minute counts into something you can read against ("two-hour days felt heavy five times of six"). Tap the same word again to clear it.

The small running time under the new/learning/due counts is a different clock from the one above — a plain stopwatch of today's total time in the study screen, not a per-card measurement and not the thing the five-second rule above is read against. It starts at zero each time you walk into the study screen, keeps running while you're actually looking at it, and pauses the moment you leave — back to the front screen, into Settings, or the app going to the background. Stepping away for a few seconds and coming straight back (a call, a notification) doesn't reset it, only leaving the screen does. What it adds up to today, across however many separate sittings, is the figure the front screen shows next to rated today.

Hard is a pass, not a failure — notice it still moves the word forward every time, just less far than Good. It is nothing like Again, which sends the word back to ten minutes.

A word you've failed before grows more slowly than one you've always got — roughly half these figures. The app tracks that per word, without you having to do anything.

Words carried over from your audit

These do not wait to appear — they arrive through the daily new-word allowance like anything else, and they come before genuinely new words. What the audit changes is the strength a word starts with: one you marked review starts about a day strong, one you marked know well starts about three weeks strong (the head start is a setting), so the app treats it as part-learned rather than new. Words you marked as known are also held back by the Known group chip, which is off by default — switch it on when you want them in the rotation.

When you fail a word you'd learned

A word due every month, failed, drops back to about two days' worth of strength and returns in ten minutes. Getting back to a month takes several sessions. That's the real cost of a lapse, and the real argument for grading honestly the first time.

5. The front screen

  • Part chips — which parts of the book are in play. Switch parts off to hold them back. Do this before you start a part, not after: switching a part off later hides words you've already begun, and they'll be overdue when you switch it back on.
  • Known / Shaky / Unseen — which of your audit groups can supply new words. Known is off by default, so you won't be shown words you already ticked. This only affects words that haven't started yet; anything already in the rotation carries on regardless.
  • Direction — Latin to English, or English to Latin. Both directions are open from the start; a reverse card arrives through its own new-word allowance, so at first the reverse direction simply has fewer words in play. (Until 2026-08-17 the reverse direction was gated behind the forward card holding for a fortnight; that gate was removed.)
  • New words today — a stepper that changes today's allowance only, without touching your normal setting.
  • Start — tells you how many words are waiting.
  • Rated today · studied — under the wordmark: how many ratings you've made today, and, once you've made at least one, how long that actually took — real wall-clock time, not a per-card estimate. See The study-screen timer in §3.
  • Calibration check (2026.08.19.2) — a one-line reminder that appears under the wordmark on 2 September 2026, two weeks after the drill layer was removed, and stays until tapped. Tapping it opens the stats page's Calibration panel (below) and clears the line; the panel's own "Remind me to look again in two weeks" sets it up again for the next checkpoint. The fortnight is the point: only ratings made after the change tell you anything about how the scheduler behaves now.
  • Study days — a dot for each of the last five weeks' days, colour-graded by how much you studied that day (switch to Retention and it grades by how well you recalled it instead), scaled to your own average rather than a fixed number. Alongside your current streak: your longest streak, the percentage of days studied since you started (or since a custom date, set via the calendar's gear icon), and a daily-average figure with its own week/month/year/all-time picker. Tap Full calendar underneath for a real month view — every day you studied still shows its three numbers right on the box (new words learnt, distinct words touched, raw ratings made), now coloured to match, and days ahead of today are shaded grey with a real forecast of how many cards will actually be due. A second view, All-time, lays your whole history out as a small scrollable grid instead of a month at a time, auto-scrolled to today. Tap any day, past or future, for its own detail — including, for a day you answered the Done screen's How did that feel?, a How it felt line — and a free-text note you can leave on it — an exam, a rough night, whatever explains the shape of the numbers that day — shown afterwards as a small dot on the calendar. A studied day's detail also opens straight into that day's actual word list on Browse, flag and bury buttons included. Days recorded before this box grew a third number show a dash there instead of a false zero.
  • Pace — how many words you've met, how many are left, the date you'd finish at your recent rate, how that lands against the start of term, and what one day off costs.
  • Where you stand — how many words are in each stage, and how often you've been right over the last month. Open Timing underneath for how quickly you've been answering.
  • Set aside — every word you've set aside, by hand or automatically, each with its meaning beside it, worst first. Tap a word's flag right there to bring it back, without waiting to meet it in a session; the note button keeps a short line about why it keeps catching you. See §9, A word keeps beating me, and Drilling your set-aside words below.

The whole syllabus

Below the pace panel is an estimate of how much of the list you would get right if you were tested now, and again on the first day of term — once assuming you stop today, once assuming you keep going at your recent rate. Words you have never met count as zero, so the number starts low and stays honest. These are the scheduler's own predictions rather than measurements; the how well the model is predicting you fold is the check on them, and it needs a few hundred real reviews before it means anything. The fuller version of that check — the same measurement broken down by interval and by button — is the Calibration panel on the stats page. (Until 2026.08.19.2 this fold was quietly leaving out every one-day interval — a word sent to "tomorrow" is granted whatever is left of today plus the night, less than a day on the clock, and the old test read that as a same-day step. It was counting about a third of your real reviews, and not the ones that go best. Fixed; both places now count the same reviews.)

The stats page

Tap the grid icon next to the gear, top left, for a fuller picture than the front screen has room for. It always answers for the whole list in whichever direction you have switched to at the top of the page — not just the parts currently in play, since the point is to see everything, including what you haven't started.

  • Where you stand — the same stages as the front screen's panel of that name, but as one bar: still to meet, in short-term steps, settling (under three weeks), holding (three weeks or more), and set aside, in proportion to the whole list.
  • Calibration (2026.08.19.2) — is the scheduler keeping its promise? Every review it sets up is a small bet: it sends a word away for a stretch and predicts you will still know it. This panel is how those bets came out, measured from your own ratings, not modelled, in whichever direction is switched on at the top, over all time or the last 30 or 14 days. By the interval you were given: for words sent away 1–6, 7–13, 14–20, 21–29, 30–44 and 45-plus study days, how often you still knew them when they came back — each bar against the mark of your own target retention, amber where it falls short, with a total row. By the button you pressed: for Hard, Good and Easy, how often the word was still known next time round, counting every press that sent it away for a study day or more (a review, or a graduation from the learning steps); Again never sets an interval, so it has nothing to test and says so. Read the rows against each other — if Hard passes about as often as Good, your Hard means Good; if Easy passes no more often than Good, the longer interval Easy buys is not being earned. Two honesty rules, deliberately: a bucket nothing has reached yet reads "not enough history yet" rather than 0% or an empty bar (the longer intervals are unreachable until a word has actually been sent that far and come back — an empty row is not a failure), and under twenty reviews the raw count is shown instead of a percentage. Under Diagnostics, folded away because it is not for everyday use: the one rule that changes what you pressed (§3) with how often it has fired, and a note that it is the only one; how you have been pressing, week by week, as a stacked bar per week — Easy should not outnumber Good; recall by whether you reviewed on time, early, late or very late (in study days — a word sent to tomorrow and reviewed tomorrow is on time whatever the clock said); recall by the time of day you rated (2026.08.19.4 — before noon, afternoon, evening, after 9 pm, a rating before 6 am counting as the night before: the same spaced reviews split by the clock, plus the share of same-day learning-step presses that were not Again, because grading late at night is both the most tired and the least reliable data the model gets — if the gap is wide, keep new words for your sharpest hours and let review fill the rest); the share of same-day repeats, the lapse rate and your median time to recall; and (2026.08.20.2) how much of that timed group revealed the answer in under three-tenths of a second — too fast to have actually tried recalling first, and worth reading against the median just before it.
  • By part — a small box for every single word, grouped by part, coloured the same way as the Where you stand bar. It fills in as words move through those stages, so you can see at a glance which parts you've made a start on and which are still untouched. Greek has no parts, so this is one block for the whole list.
  • Drill (2026-08-15) — how the set-aside pile is doing on its own terms, separately from the stages above, since drilling never touches your real scheduling. How many words are currently set aside and how long the oldest one has been sitting there; how many of them haven't actually been drilled yet, and how many carry a note; how many drill sessions you've run and how accurate the first look at a word was, last five sessions against all-time; and how the pile has moved over the last two weeks — words newly set aside against words brought back.

Browsing the full list

"Browse the full list" under The whole syllabus on the front screen opens every word in the deck at once, in either direction, grouped by part and alphabetised within each part. A search box narrows it to whatever you type — matched against both sides of every card (2026-08-16), so a Latin or Greek word finds its row from the English direction and an English gloss finds it from the other; accents, breathings and macrons are ignored, and a final sigma matches a middle one. In the Greek deck the box types Greek from an ordinary Latin keyboard, on the same scheme as the Hoplite keyboard: a gives α, u θ, j ξ, c ψ, v ω, w final ς, and the digits 1–9 add a rough breathing, smooth breathing, acute, grave, circumflex, macron, breve, iota subscript or diaeresis to the letter you just typed (press again to remove it). The αβγ/abc button beside the box switches that off for typing English; a real Greek keyboard, such as the phone's own, is left alone either way. The same scheme, with the same toggle, is on Drill's typed English→Greek answer box. The row filters underneath narrow by how well you know each word, the same stages as everywhere else in the app. Each part starts collapsed; tap its heading to open it, and tap any word to reveal its answer in place — this never counts as a review and never touches your scheduling, it's just a look-up. The little dot on each row is the same colour language as the stats page's boxes. For any word you've met at least once, revealing it also shows how long it's taken to learn: how many reviews and how long ago the first one was, plus a rough estimate of the time you've actually spent thinking about it (based on how long you paused before revealing the answer each time — reviews where that reading looked unreliable, for instance because you'd looked away, aren't counted, and it says how many of the total that leaves). Every row also carries its own flag (2026-08-08) — tap it to set the word aside or bring it back on the spot, the same action as everywhere else in the app, without waiting to meet it in a session or to trip the automatic set-aside threshold first. A second icon, the clock, buries the word for today only — a lighter, temporary skip that returns to normal rotation on its own the next study day, with no separate step to bring it back, for "not this word right now" without the commitment of flagging it. Checkboxes on each row let you select several at once for a bulk-action bar that flags or buries the lot together. Arriving here by tapping one of the calendar's own days on the front screen filters this list down to just the words touched that day, with a bar across the top to clear the filter and see everything again.

Drilling your set-aside words

"Drill these words" under the Set aside list on the front screen opens with a choice (2026-08-15): check the whole set-aside pile in one pass, same as this always did, or run a shorter focus session instead — 10 to 25 words, picked with a slider that remembers where you last left it. A focus session pulls from whichever words haven't been drilled recently first, so repeated short sessions still work through the whole pile eventually rather than always landing on the same handful; a look-alike pair (see below) is always kept whole, never split across sessions. Either way, nothing here touches your real scheduling — answer it right, answer it wrong, drill the same words again a minute later, it costs nothing.

Multiple-choice was the original version of this and is gone as of 2026-08-08 (too easy to get right by elimination without actually knowing the word); what each word gets instead now depends on direction. English to Latin/Greek always gets a plain typed-answer box — type the word, and it's checked leniently: case, spacing, and accents/macrons are all forgiving, since the point is whether you know the word, not whether you reproduced an accent mark exactly. If the word you're up against has more than one right translation, typing any one of them counts. Latin/Greek to English is a plain flashcard instead (changed 2026-08-15, back from an earlier typed version): tap to show the meaning, then judge yourself — "Knew it" or "Didn't know it" — since typing out an English gloss by hand wasn't testing anything the reveal doesn't already, and only slowed things down; if the gloss lists more than one synonym, that's shown in full, same as ever. When two or more of today's set-aside words look alike — close spelling, not merely both being hard — they're pulled together into their own round instead, regardless of direction: every word in that little group and its meanings appear at once, shuffled, and you tap to match each word to its meaning, getting the actual distinction between them drilled directly rather than being tested on them one at a time as if they weren't easily confused with each other. A wrong pairing just resets so you can try again — it doesn't end the round.

A "Bring this word(s) back to normal review" button lets you graduate a word (or, mid-match-round, everything in that group at once) the moment it's stopped tripping you up, the same action as the flag, from a different screen. A small list icon at the top of the drill screen switches to a plain, ungraded list of everything you've set aside with its meaning right there — for just reading over rather than being tested — and switches back to practice without losing your place. Each word in that list also carries a small note button (2026-08-15): a short line for anything worth remembering about it — why it keeps getting confused, a mnemonic, the word it gets mixed up with — added or edited there, or from the Set-aside list on the front screen itself; either place shows the same note. It's deliberately never shown during an actual practice round, only in these two ungraded places, so it can't turn a retrieval test into an open-book one.

6. A sensible daily routine

Open it once a day and clear what's due. That's it. The app is designed around the assumption that you show up daily; if you skip days the work doesn't vanish, it piles up.

  • Do the reviews before the new words. The app already orders it that way. If a day is going to be short, doing reviews and no new words is far better than the reverse.
  • If you fall behind, don't try to clear everything at once. Set new words to 0 for a few days and just work through the backlog.
  • Watch the "right but slow" count in the Timing panel. Those are the words about to fail, before they fail.
  • Remember the tail. New words you add today generate reviews for months. Adding 50 a day through September means carrying a few hundred reviews a day through Michaelmas. If you want term to be lighter, ease off the new words in September rather than after term starts.

Tap targets

The buttons are bigger than they look — the invisible tappable area extends well above each one and into the gaps, so you don't need to aim. Tapping anywhere on the card reveals the answer, and for a fraction of a second afterwards the rating buttons ignore taps, so a reveal can't accidentally grade the card. The same fraction of a second runs the other way too: right after a new card appears, its own "show answer" button ignores taps for a moment, so the tail end of a fast double-tap on the previous card's rating can't flash the next word's answer before you meant to see it.

Made a mistake? The arrow at the top right undoes the last rating, up to forty back. You may not need it: the app also recaps the word you just rated, what you pressed, and how far it jumped forward — often enough to answer "what did I just do" without undoing anything. On a wide screen that is a small card in the top-right corner; on a phone held upright it is a single line along the bottom of the card, just above the buttons (added 2026.08.20.4 — before that, a phone had no recap at all). It never moves the word, and tapping it just reveals the answer like tapping anywhere else on the card does.

On a keyboard — a Mac or an iPad with one attached — Space or Enter reveals the answer, and once it's showing, Space or Enter again rates it Easy (changed 2026-08-13 from Good) — the fastest path through a word that needed no thought at all. The number keys rate the word — 1 Again, 2 Hard, 3 Good, 4 Easy, the same four on every word (until 2026.08.19.3 a word still being learned had no Hard button and 2/3 meant Good/Easy there; that shifting is gone) — and Z or 0 undoes (Shift with Z undoes too). F flags or unflags the word you're looking at, silently, with no toast (see §9), and K keeps a word in when it is one "Again" away from setting itself aside — also §9. With Quick rate switched on (§3), 4 also works before you reveal, on a settled word, and grades it Easy on the spot. On a wide screen these can optionally be written right on the buttons themselves — see "Study screen, wide layout" in section 8. §10 has every key on every screen in one place, if you just want the list.

7. Backing up — read this one

Everything lives on the device you are using. There is no account and no server. Losing it means losing every interval the app has learned about you, which is worth much more than the word list itself.

Three things protect you, in increasing order of reliability:

  • It saves after every single rating, into the browser's own database, and checks the save was actually issued. The review log is only ever added to, never rewritten, and everything else is written as one bounded record, so saving stays quick however long the log grows. If it ever fails you'll see a red warning on the front screen — act on it.
  • Backup files are saved by hand, never automatically. Safari silently blocks a download no tap asked for, so an automatic file backup could never be trusted — there isn't one. Use Export a backup file in settings before anything risky; and when sync is off or has gone quiet and fifty ratings have built up, a Back up now button appears in settings too. On iPhone the file lands in Files → Downloads.
  • Sync is the primary backup. One short sync code (ten letters and digits, like ABCDE-FGHJK) is the whole setup: get one from Get a code for this device — in settings, or once on the launcher page in front of both decks, see below — and every save is pushed to the sync server a few seconds later, at most once a minute during a sitting and once more when you put the app away; each launch fetches whatever the other copy left and merges it in (2026.08.19.5 — see below), so two copies can no longer overwrite each other. The front screen shows how long ago that last worked, and turns amber then red as it ages. The code never leaves your device: it is turned into a lookup key and an encryption key there, and only those reach the server, which stores an encrypted copy it cannot read. Treat the code like a password — anyone holding it can read and overwrite your copy. One code serves both apps: each deck keeps its own slot under it.

Using it on more than one device. Give each device its own name first, in This device's name — pick from the dropdown, or Other… for anything not listed — then type the same sync code into every device you want kept in step. Each device pushes a few seconds after you stop rating, and fetches by itself when you open the app or return to it from the background, so the iPad picks up what you did on the phone without your having to think about it. Since 2026.08.19.5 the two copies are merged, the way Anki merges, rather than one replacing the other. The rules, in plain words: every rating from both copies is kept — the review histories are combined, and a rating is never lost to a sync; a word you happened to rate on both copies since they last agreed keeps whichever copy's rating came later, with both ratings still in its history; a rating you undid stays undone even if the other copy had already seen it; the day's counts are recounted from the combined ratings, and study time is the sum of what each copy measured; each setting travels from whichever copy changed it more recently (the direction you are studying and the calibration reminder included) — only This device's name stays local. It happens quietly, with a one-line summary afterwards — “Merged — 12 reviews from iPhone · Safari, 30 from iPhone · Home Screen; 3 words rated on both, later rating kept” — and Settings keeps the last such line under the sync code. Each copy of the app labels its own ratings with what it is — the typed name plus Home Screen, Safari, Chrome and so on — so two copies both called “iPhone” no longer read as one; that is also what the summary names. The old “this device has 2 reviews the synced copy does not have…” question is gone for ordinary use; it only reappears if the merge itself fails, and then Cancel still changes nothing. A device typing in a sync code for the very first time — pairing a fresh Home Screen icon, most often — still looks at what the code holds before it pushes anything, and merges with it rather than writing over it. Pull and merge in settings does the same fetch-and-merge by hand; Push now sends this device's copy up as it is. One case to know about: studying on a device before ever typing in its sync code — that device then has its own ratings, and pairing it merges them in like any others, so nothing is lost there either; but its settings block will be whichever is newer, so check them once after pairing.

The safety net. Two spares exist so that any sync, merge or restore can be undone once. This device keeps the copy it last replaced — by a merge, a pull, a file restore, or a restore — and Restore the copy this replaced in settings brings it back (the copy it displaces becomes the new spare, so the step is reversible too). The sync server keeps one spare per deck: the last copy pushed by a different copy of the app than the one that pushed last — it is written only when one copy takes over from another, not on every push, because the server's free write budget is shared with everything else — and Restore the other copy's last push fetches it. A restored copy wins everywhere: its word states and settings take over on every device at the next sync; no ratings are lost, because the other copy's ratings merge back in as ratings. The sync server's answers also carry its own clock, and if this device's clock is more than five minutes off you are told once — ratings are ordered by the clock, and a hand-set clock would order them wrongly.

Adding a device. There is nothing to copy across but the code itself: Copy code on the launcher page puts it on the clipboard, and typing it into the new device's launcher (or a deck's settings) is the whole of pairing. If the device you're pairing already has a code of its own, it asks before switching, rather than replacing it quietly; Cancel there changes nothing at all. Changing the code on a device does not delete anything anywhere — the old slot stays as it was for any device still using it.

The launcher also shows your progress, not just links. Each deck's card names how many words are ready to study right now, once you've opened that deck at least once since this shipped. Underneath, an Activity section shows a study streak — combined across both decks, with each deck's own count alongside — and a small scrolling calendar per deck sharing one timeline, so a gap in one is easy to spot against the other. Tap a deck's name there to show or hide its row; nothing about what it tracks changes either way.

Install it to your Home Screen. Open it in Safari, tap Share, then Add to Home Screen, and use it from that icon rather than from a browser tab. Safari deletes stored data for websites you haven't visited in a week; installed apps are exempt. This matters more than anything else on this page.

Two ways to do this. Pin the launcher page (the one with the Latin/Greek links) and open both decks from its links — they share one Home Screen icon and, because of that, share one copy of the device name and sync code: fill them in once on the launcher and both decks pick them up. Or pin Latin and Greek as two separate icons — each is then its own isolated app as far as the phone is concerned, so each keeps and needs its own copy of the code and device name. Either is fine; the launcher just removes some retyping if you use both decks regularly. Since the 2026.08.16.7 build the two decks are one page, deck.html?deck=latin and deck.html?deck=greek, so an icon added before then (pointing at the old latin.html/greek.html) needs removing and adding again from the new address — the data on the device is untouched by that.

The icon is a capital V in the same Palatino the wordmark uses, on the vellum theme's own paper colour. iOS and macOS copy this the instant you add it and never check back for a new one — there's no way for an installed web app to update its own icon later, on either platform.

Checking an import before you keep it

A parser gets a page wrong quietly. One column boundary read a word too far, and every gloss on that page becomes the wrong answer — and a wrong card doesn't sit still: it gets scheduled, and you rehearse it until you notice. So the preview is worth reading, and reading past the first screen. On a long import it now shows three windows — the start, the middle and the end — because the top of a list is where the front matter is, while a boundary that drifts does so somewhere in the middle. The count line above the table says how many rows it is showing of how many, and every line the parser refused is listed underneath, with the reason.

Check before you keep, rather than after: once a word is in a deck you can edit its term, set it aside, or delete it (tap it in Browse), but there is no way to edit a gloss in place — a mis-split gloss has to be deleted and added again.

When a page defeats it: converting with an AI first

Some pages have nothing to read — a scan, a photograph — and some come out wrong however the separator is set. One route is to hand the page to a chat assistant, have it produce plain tab-separated lines, read what comes back against the page, and bring that text back here. Copy a prompt for an AI on the import screen puts the instruction below on the clipboard.

Know which way round the two fail. This parser fails loudly: rows go missing, columns come out visibly wrong, and it lists every line it skipped. A model fails quietly — an invented or silently "corrected" gloss reads exactly like a real one. That is why the prompt forbids invention and makes it declare anything it could not read, and why the output is worth checking against the page rather than merely read for plausibility.

Turn this vocabulary list into tab-separated lines -- one entry per line, in this shape:

term<TAB>gloss

Rules:
- Copy exactly what is printed. Do not translate, correct, modernise, expand abbreviations, or add anything that is not on the page.
- Keep accents, breathings and macrons exactly as printed.
- Keep grammatical information (principal parts, genitive endings, gender, "+ acc") with the term, in the order printed.
- One printed entry is one line. Where a page has two columns of entries side by side, read the whole of one column before starting the other.
- If the list is divided into parts, chapters or lessons, put that name in a third tab-separated column.
- If you cannot read an entry with confidence, leave it out and list it at the end under a line reading SKIPPED.
- Output nothing else: no commentary, no headings, no numbering, no markdown.

9. If something looks wrong

English to Latin is empty

Check the direction's own new-word allowance in Settings — the reverse direction has its own number, and 0 there means nothing new is introduced that way. (The old fortnight gate on reverse cards was removed 2026-08-17; nothing is held back on that account any more.)

I've suddenly got hundreds due

Either you missed some days, or a big batch you learned together has come due together. Set new words to 0 until it clears. Don't skip the backlog — those are the words at the exact moment they're worth reviewing.

An interval looks far too long

A long gap is only ever given to a word the app has good evidence you know. If it genuinely feels wrong, press a lower grade next time you see it — that's the correction mechanism, and it works immediately. If it feels wrong across the board, don't guess — the stats page's Calibration panel (§5) shows, interval by interval, whether words sent away that far are actually coming back known at your target rate. If a bucket with a few hundred reviews behind it sits well under the mark, raise target retention a little; if it doesn't, the intervals are right and the feeling isn't.

I've been away a fortnight

Just start again. Overdue words are handled properly — the app knows how long it's been, and getting one right after a long gap counts for more than getting it right on time.

A word keeps beating me

If a word is beating you badly in one sitting, that's worth listening to — a flag icon appears on the card once Again has landed on it 2 times in a single study day, which you can tap to set it aside by hand; it's a real tap target, not a fiddly little glyph. Past a threshold you set in Settings (5 to 15 Agains in a day, default 12) it happens on its own — at that point the answer isn't more repetitions, it's learning the word some other way, then bringing it back when you're ready. You get one turn's warning before that, and the last word on it: once a word is one Again away from the threshold, a small amber warning mark appears on the card next to the flag, and tapping it — or pressing K — keeps that word in for the rest of the study day, so the next failure doesn't park it. That's there because the count can't tell apart a word that simply isn't coming from one where you've got three of its four meanings and it's nearly yours. The override lasts the study day and no longer, on purpose — the count behind the set-aside is itself same-day, so tomorrow starts at zero and the warning is live again; a word you find yourself rescuing several days running is one the threshold is probably right about. Press it a second time to let the word go after all, and if it's already past the threshold by then it's set aside at once rather than quietly at the next reload. The mark never appears on a word you've already set aside — the flag is still what brings those back. Changing that threshold only ever looks forward: raising it never brings an already-set-aside word back by itself, and lowering it never reaches back to sweep aside a word based on presses already logged earlier the same day — a fresh press today can trip the new number at once, but the passive backlog can't, and the new number is only in full, unqualified force from your next study day. The front screen's Set aside section lists what you have actually set aside — by hand or automatically — each with its meaning beside it, so you can see the whole pile without waiting to meet each word in a session. (It used to also list every word at 5+ Agains today under a heading that described only that half; the mixed list was removed rather than relabelled, so what it shows and what it is called are now the same thing.) On a keyboard, F flags or unflags the word on screen straight away, at the same 2-Again threshold as the icon, quietly and with no toast — tapping the icon itself does toast, same as everywhere else in the app. Once a word is set aside, Drilling your set-aside words above is a way to work on it directly, as often as you like, without it affecting your real scheduling.

The counts changed when I toggled something

Part and group chips filter which words can start. They never hide a word already in the rotation, and they never affect anything you've already rated.

How do I know which version I'm running?

The version sits at the bottom of the front screen. Tap it for the list of what changed. If the front screen has no whole syllabus section, you are on an older file. The launcher page (the one with the Latin/Greek links) shows the same version at its foot too, and tapping it jumps straight to that same list — no need to open a deck first.
